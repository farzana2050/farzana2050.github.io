# Computer Science ePortfolio

## Professional Self-Assessment

Welcome to my Computer Science ePortfolio. The Computer Science program and the creation of this ePortfolio have enabled me to realize my growth as a problem solver, software developer, and emerging computing professional. In the program, I've come to understand that it takes more than running a program to be a good computer scientist. It involves grasping user and organizational requirements, choosing the right algorithms and data structures, creating maintainable software, managing databases, explaining technical choices, and being security-conscious throughout the development process. My coursework also helped me develop my professional goal of software development, full-stack development, as I enjoy combining programming, data management, user-facing functionality, and problem-solving to create complete solutions. The ePortfolio has enabled me to share these skills as a set of integrated capabilities and demonstrate how I can build upon previous work, look at the gaps, and enhance the skills with those acquired later in the program.
The program helped me improve my collaborative and communication skills in the professional computing environment. I learned to consider software development through the eyes of the technical team members and stakeholders in the software development life-cycle concepts, planning, requirements, testing, and project documentation. I learned that working together requires shared expectations, readable code, helpful documentation, feedback, and the ability to explain technical concepts at the right level for the audience. In individual projects, I needed to understand the requirements, break them down into manageable tasks, test if it behaves as required, and communicate the results in written reports, code comments, demonstrations, and presentations. The experiences helped me to get better at communicating technical information effectively and got me ready to work on development teams that have programmers, testers, managers, clients, and other team members who have varying priorities and technical expertise.
My technical studies built up a base of knowledge in data structures and algorithms, software engineering, and databases. I learned to consider solutions not just for their ability to yield the desired output, but also for their efficiency, maintainability, scalability, and design trade-offs. Using algorithms taught me to divide up a problem into smaller steps, to think about state and decision making, and how to select structures to facilitate the operations I needed to perform. I learned a lot about modular design, object-oriented programming, testing and debugging, user-interface development, and how to divide responsibilities within an application in software engineering. Those skills were expanded through database coursework, which taught me how to store, retrieve, update, validate, and protect persistent information via applications. Using both relational and NoSQL concepts gave me a sense of the importance of data design as part of the application design and not just a back-end issue.
Security is another key consideration when I'm assessing software. Another part of the programme I spent more time on was functionality, but later coursework and the capstone project have made me think more about how it might be misused, how to secure credentials and sensitive data, how to validate data, how to handle failures safely, and how to avoid making decisions that could create unnecessary vulnerabilities. I now think about software security from a perspective that security is a concern from the beginning of design and development and not at the end. This attitude is particularly crucial for applications that rely on databases and are connected to networks, as they can be vulnerable to improper input validation, exposure of credentials, or overly permissive operations. I will keep building this security mentality when I get to work professionally in software development.
The artifacts in this portfolio illustrate a variety of skills that I acquired during the program. The software design and engineering enhancement demonstrates my ability to enhance an Android event-tracking application by improving its structure, functionality, validation, and usability.
The algorithms & data structures enhancement illustrates problem-solving by creating an intelligent agent called Treasure Hunt, which learns the most efficient way to navigate a maze using reinforcement learning, experience replay, neural network training, and systematic testing. The database enhancement proves my capacity to enhance a CRUD application in MongoDB with better validation, secure credential management, connection checking, defensive update and delete operations, and full create-read-update-delete testing. As a whole, these artifacts demonstrate the transition from creating functional programs to assessing current solutions, identifying risks and weaknesses, using more complex techniques, and ensuring that improvements are effective.
In general, the Computer Science program has equipped me with a wider understanding of how software, algorithms, data, communication, collaboration, and security work together to enter the field. I feel more comfortable learning new tools, fixing bugs, assessing design decisions, explaining my thinking, and refining software, rather than creating new software. My ePortfolio is a demonstration of my technical skills and development: it is a reflection of what I am able to create right now, but it is also a reflection of the process I follow when analyzing, testing, improving, and communicating computing solutions. While seeking software or full stack development opportunities, I plan to keep developing these skills with work experience, further education, and applications that are useful and dependable to users and organizations.
